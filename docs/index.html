Can machine learning prioritize content pages for review?

A client-grouped evaluation of historical search-performance signals for ranking pages that may warrant human investigation.

Abstract

Question: Can a machine-learning ranking system prioritize content pages for review more effectively than a fixed rule baseline when tested on clients excluded from training? Data: The analysis joined content metadata with query-level performance aggregated to 133,852 client–content pages, using a snapshot covering 2 April to 30 June 2026. Method: A Random Forest and Logistic Regression were evaluated with Precision@K on a single 80/20 client-grouped split, with a target defined as a click decline of at least 20% between two 30-day windows. Result: The Random Forest achieved Precision@100 of 0.790, Precision@500 of 0.780, and Precision@1,000 of 0.737; the Week 4 rule baseline scored 0.060, 0.062, and 0.031 on the same held-out set, while the test base rate was 0.106. Use: These results support further evaluation of a human-review prioritization workflow, not a claim that the model forecasts future decline or that refreshing a page causes better performance.

1. Introduction / Problem statement

Content teams often have more pages to inspect than they can review at once. The practical question is not simply whether a page has a problem; it is which pages deserve attention first, based on available evidence.

This project tests a ranking approach for content opportunity review. It uses historical search and content signals to order pages for manual inspection, then compares that ranking with a rule-based score under a client-held-out evaluation.

The study deliberately defines a narrow target: observed click decline across two 30-day windows. That target is a measurable proxy for triage, not a direct measure of content quality, customer value, or refresh success.

2. Data

Source and release

The project used the FlyRank internship warehouse dataset, loaded through the Hugging Face dataset configurations dim_content and fact_content_query_90d. The available executed notebook outputs report 519,606 rows and 26 columns in the content dimension, and 2,414,248 rows and 21 columns in the query-performance fact table.

The performance snapshot covers 2026-04-02 through 2026-06-30. The fact table includes 90-day totals and separate previous-30-day and latest-30-day metrics. Query-level records were aggregated by client and content identifier, then joined to content metadata, yielding 133,852 page-level rows and 40 columns before model-specific preparation.

Unit of analysis and public-safe handling

  • Unit: one row per client–content page after query-level aggregation.
  • Target: a binary proxy for a 20% or greater click decline from the previous 30-day window to the latest 30-day window.
  • Excluded from the public paper: raw queries, page URLs, client names, and row-level client/content identifiers. No row-level operational queue is embedded in this page.
  • Excluded from model features: latest-30-day and 90-day outcome-period performance columns, as they overlap the period used to define the label. Identifiers are used for grouping, not as predictive features.
  • Separate sample audit: a 200-row sample contained a trend_direction field that matched its label row-for-row; it was excluded from modeling. That sample is not treated as the primary capstone dataset.
Data interpretation: the notebooks do not establish that the target captures every meaningful decline. Pages with zero clicks in the previous window are assigned a negative label by the implementation, while percentage changes for pages with very few previous clicks can be unstable.

3. Methodology

Target definition

For pages with a nonzero previous-period click count, the proxy percentage change is:

(clicks_last30 − clicks_prev30) / clicks_prev30

The positive class is assigned when this value is less than or equal to −0.20. In the executed implementation, zero-denominator values become missing during the calculation and then receive label 0 when the binary target is constructed. This choice is a limitation, not evidence that those pages are healthy.

Features and preprocessing

The seven model features were impressions_prev30, clicks_prev30, avg_position_prev30, word_count, search_volume, keyword_char_count, and url_char_count. Missing values were imputed with medians fitted on the training set and applied to the test set. Logistic Regression used standardized features; Random Forest used the original feature scales.

Models and baseline

  • Random Forest: 100 trees, maximum depth 6, random state 42.
  • Logistic Regression: maximum 1,000 iterations, random state 42, with standard scaling.
  • Week 4 rule baseline: a fixed threshold score based on 90-day average position, CTR, and impressions. It was reconstructed on the same grouped test rows, but it was originally designed for weak visibility rather than the click-decline target, and its 90-day inputs overlap the outcome period. Therefore, it is a useful historical reference but not a fully matched, leakage-safe benchmark.

Validation design and leakage checks

The primary split used GroupShuffleSplit with 20% held out and random_state=42, grouping by client_hash_id. The split produced 117,944 training pages and 15,908 test pages with zero client overlap. This evaluates ranking on clients absent from training, which is closer to the intended cross-client use than a random-row split.

Latest-30-day and 90-day outcome-period metrics were excluded from the model feature set. Identifiers were used for grouping and traceability only. The validation audit also found that a random-row split had 50 clients represented in both train and test; its higher scores are reported as a diagnostic, not as the primary result. A single grouped split across 52 clients is still limited evidence, and does not replace repeated grouped splits or time-based validation.

4. Results

The primary result is the executed Week 5 comparison on the client-grouped test set of 15,908 pages, where the positive-label base rate was 10.6%.

Figure 1. Precision@K by ranking method

Takeaway: Random Forest ranked the decline proxy above the other methods at each cutoff in this held-out split, but the rule baseline's different objective and overlapping 90-day inputs limit the strength of the comparison.

Figure 2. Random-row vs client-grouped validation

Takeaway: Precision@100 was 89.0% on a random-row split and 79.0% on the client-grouped split; the random-row split placed 50 clients in both sets, while the grouped split had zero client overlap. Test-set sizes and base rates also differed, so the gap cannot be attributed to client leakage alone.

Interpretation

Random Forest's Precision@K was 0.790, 0.780, and 0.737 on the grouped test set, compared with a 0.106 positive-label rate. This indicates useful ranking signal on this split. It does not establish future forecasting performance, stable performance across future samples, or a causal benefit from editing pages.

The Week 5 feature-importance output assigns 77.68% of Random Forest importance to clicks_prev30, followed by 16.63% to impressions_prev30. Because previous clicks are also the denominator in the target, the model may partly exploit the target's mathematical structure. This makes low-volume handling a priority for further validation.

5. Limitations & honest framing

  • Proxy outcome: the label measures observed historical click decline, not content quality, satisfaction, future decline, or refresh success.
  • Low-volume instability: a percentage decline can be extreme when the previous count is one or two clicks. The audited false-positive examples included very low historical click counts.
  • Mechanical target relationship: clicks_prev30 is the target denominator and the most important feature. A traffic floor such as at least 10 previous clicks was proposed, but the reported model was not retrained with that change.
  • Baseline comparability: the Week 4 baseline was designed for weak visibility, not the decline proxy, and uses 90-day inputs that overlap the outcome period. Its scores should not be framed as a fully matched, leakage-safe baseline.
  • One grouped split: validation held out clients and avoided overlap, but represents a single split across 52 clients. Repeated grouped splits and a later time window are needed.
  • Not a future forecast: both 30-day windows are inside the snapshot ending 30 June 2026; the task classifies an observed decline rather than predicting a later one.
  • Separate reported comparison: the 781-record results and manually entered Precision@K chart in the draft were not independently reproduced from predictions and labels. They are not used as verified evidence in this page.
  • Operational impact not measured: no experiment here establishes that taking action on a recommended page improves clicks, impressions, rankings, or revenue.
Honest conclusion: the evidence supports testing the model as a human-review prioritization aid. It does not justify automatic content changes or causal business-impact claims.

6. Ranked recommendations / action playbook

The Week 7 queue contains 15,908 unique pages from the client-grouped held-out set. It was scored by a refit of the model configuration; Week 5 Precision@K values describe the earlier evaluated fit and were not recomputed on the Week 7 queue. The action categories are workflow tiers, not estimates of business value.

Figure 3. Week 7 action categories

Takeaway: Half of the held-out queue is marked for no immediate action, while 25.0% are marked for monitoring, 23.3% for review, and 1.7% for high-priority review. Percentile-based tiers structure workload; they do not prove which pages will benefit from a refresh.

Priority Rule in the playbook Recommended handling
REVIEW_HIGH Score at or above the 90th percentile and previous-period clicks ≥ 10 Review early; confirm the evidence and potential value before changing anything.
REVIEW Score at or above the 75th percentile, including top-decile pages below the traffic floor Inspect content fit, search intent, technical signals, seasonality, and recent changes.
MONITOR Score at or above the median and below the 75th percentile Track subsequent performance and revisit if signals change.
NO_IMMEDIATE_ACTION Score below the median Defer routine review unless independent evidence warrants attention.

Recommended reviewer order

  1. Start with REVIEW_HIGH, then REVIEW, while checking previous-period traffic volume.
  2. Inspect current search results, intent match, content quality, recent edits, tracking or technical issues, and possible seasonality.
  3. Use the model score as one signal only. Record the human decision and reason.
  4. If a refresh is approved, measure outcomes against a suitable comparison or controlled rollout rather than assuming the change caused any movement.
  5. Monitor Precision@K on new labeled data, label prevalence, feature distributions, missingness, client mix, and action-tier distributions. Consider retraining if performance or data conditions materially change.
No-go list: do not automatically publish, rewrite, delete, redirect, canonicalize, or otherwise modify pages based on this score alone. Do not label content as low quality without human inspection.

Key configuration

  • Grouped split: 80/20 by client, random_state=42.
  • Random Forest: n_estimators=100, max_depth=6, random_state=42.
  • Logistic Regression: max_iter=1000, random_state=42; standard scaling fitted on training data.
  • Missing-value imputation: per-feature median fitted on training data only.
  • Primary metric: Precision@K for K = 100, 500, and 1,000.

For exact reproduction, run the notebooks in order in an environment with access to the internship dataset and required dependencies. Do not publish credentials, raw private data, or row-level action queues. The public page intentionally avoids exposing row-level identifiers.
